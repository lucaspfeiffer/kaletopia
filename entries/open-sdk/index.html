Open SDK

A software development kit from the restaurant technology company Open that gives every restaurant a codebase of its own, built by coding agents on a shared foundation.

By Lucas Pfeiffer and Claude

October 8, 20262 min read

Open is a company that builds ordering technology for restaurants. In May 2026 it described a change in how it does that, which it calls the Open SDK. The idea fits in a sentence from its own story page: "Every restaurant gets a codebase of its own. The SDK carries the parts that should be the same everywhere, and the agents make the rest specific to the brand, the concept, and the customer."

How it got here

Through the 2010s, restaurant sales moved online, mostly through marketplaces like DoorDash and Uber Eats. Those marketplaces are good at what they do and expensive for the restaurant, so restaurants want a direct channel of their own: a website, a mobile app, a kiosk, and the tools around them, like promotions, loyalty, and messaging.

Open's story lays out three ways a restaurant could build that channel before, and why each fell short on one of three things a direct channel needs: a great experience, personalization to the brand, and being within reach for a restaurant's budget. Build it in-house and it is personal but out of reach. Have an agency build it and it is personal but underinvested. Use a standardized product and it is within reach but looks like everyone else's.

Open began as the third kind: one shared product, configured per restaurant. The story is candid about what that gave and what it could not. Then, as the page puts it, "at the end of 2025, coding agents hit an inflection point."

What the SDK is

The analogy the page reaches for is the Google Maps SDK: a kit that an app includes so it does not have to build maps itself. The Open SDK is that for restaurant ordering. It carries ordering, catering, receipts, campaigns, analytics, agentic testing, and automatic version upgrades. Around it, coding agents build the restaurant's own codebase, taking edits from the brand, the merchant, and Open, so the marketing site, the ordering flow, and the app can be specific to one restaurant without a team of engineers.

Three ways to change what gets built are described: a developer platform for those who want to code, self-service edits for those who do not, and design as a service for those who want it done. A version upgrade is one command. So is scaling from two stores to two thousand.

The claim

The page closes on a promise about invisibility: "Restaurants using the Open SDK will have the best restaurant ordering experiences, but customers will never know it's powered by the Open SDK, and that's how it should be." And on ownership: the SDK is "designed to give restaurants ownership over their technology."

The story page carries a note that nothing is asserted on it that has not been said out loud, which is the standard this entry holds to as well.
